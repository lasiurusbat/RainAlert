Rain Alert design directions

Three ways to make the forecast read faster

Rain Alert answers one question, and the reader asks it from the doorway: will I get wet, and when? A person on a phone at 7:40 needs a single number and a single time. A person at a laptop needs to see the shape of the week. The three directions below answer that at three different distances: Night street for the person on the move, The instrument for the person who wants the shape of the week, The almanac for the person who likes a printed table. Every direction below keeps the photograph, keeps the honest band names, and reorganises the page around the moment the question is asked.

The background photograph is unchanged in all three. It is a wet street at dusk, and everything below takes its palette from that picture: wet asphalt, sodium lamps, tail-light red, the haze in the middle distance. Nothing here introduces a stock palette.

01

Where the app is today

A white card on a dark photograph. The search box and the preferences row sit above the forecast, so on a phone the answer is below the fold. The hourly strip is 38 pixels tall and its values live in a hover tooltip, which a touch screen never shows. The week is seven rows of nearly identical text.

02

A new logo: the umbrella, reduced to one stroke

The current mark is a blue rounded square with a white droplet, which is the most common shape on the internet for a rain app, and it repeats the ☔ emoji that already appears in the daily rows. The new mark is the app's own advice drawn once: a canopy, a centre shaft, a hooked handle. The silhouette is a hook and an arc, which never reads as a teardrop at 16 pixels.

Rules the mark keeps

  • Two files, never one recoloured file. A browser caches a favicon by its URL, so the quiet and the rain icon have to live at two addresses.
  • The two states differ in shape as well as colour. Rain adds two strokes to the silhouette, so the icons are told apart at 16 pixels without relying on hue.
  • The mark replaces the 🌧️ in the heading and the ☔ in the rows. Emoji render differently on every platform, and the rows already carry a written severity for screen readers.
  • tools/make_icons.py has the old droplet hard-coded in it. Adopting this mark means replacing that constant and regenerating the four PNGs.
  • The wordmark is the mark plus the name set in the direction's own display face, never an image, so it stays selectable and it scales.

Why the hook

A droplet is the shape of the weather. A hook is the shape of the decision: the reader is carrying something. The mark names the app's advice, which is the same advice the banner, the title and the calendar export give.

What it must not do

  • Carry the severity on its own. The favicon switches on the umbrella threshold, and the threshold is a server setting. The mark is a brand, the rain state is the alert.
  • Lose the plate. The installed icon has to fill its canvas because the platform crops it, and the maskable variant keeps the middle 80% clear.
  • Depend on a gradient or a blur. At 16 pixels there is room for a silhouette and nothing else.
03

Night street

The card stops being a white slab and becomes part of the photograph: a dark panel of the same night the photo shows, lit by the same sodium lamps. The forecast is reordered by the order people ask in it, right now, then the rest of today, then the week.

What it changes in the experience

  • The answer moves to the top. The city and the sentence about today are the first thing on the page, and the search field is one compact input in the top bar. A reader on a phone no longer scrolls past a form to find out whether to take an umbrella.
  • The hourly strip grows from 38 pixels to 62 and gets values without a hover. The hours that have already passed are dimmed, so the strip reads as the rest of the day rather than as a chart of the whole day.
  • Now becomes a labelled rule instead of an 8 pixel dot, and the hours after it are the bright ones. That answers the only question that matters at 7:40.
  • The week becomes seven columns of figures. The eye reads a column of numbers instead of seven rows of sentences, and the two days that reach the threshold carry an amber rule under them.
  • One accent colour. Amber means wet, blue means possible, grey means unlikely. Nothing else in the panel is coloured, so colour keeps carrying one meaning.

What it costs

  • The dark panel is a bigger contrast job. Every figure has to clear 4.5 to 1 against #0D1216, and the three band colours have to stay apart in the dark.
  • Amber on a dark ground reads as warm, which is right for this photograph, but the tab favicon and the social card would need the same treatment or the page and the card stop being the same object.
  • The card grows. It has to stay inside a 768 pixel window, and the strip plus the week row have to be paid for out of the padding that the headline and the donation line currently take.
04

The instrument

A forecast is a measurement, so the page is a measuring instrument. The week is one continuous curve with the umbrella threshold drawn across it as a rule, and the reader's own threshold is a second rule on the same axis. The only question the chart asks is whether a point sits above the line or below it.

What it changes in the experience

  • The shape of a week becomes one glance. Seven rows cannot tell you that Tuesday and Thursday are wet and the rest is dry. A curve can, before you read a single figure.
  • The reader's own threshold stops being a small triangle on a row and becomes a line on the chart. The days their line catches are visible in the same picture as the days the umbrella catches.
  • The hourly strip and the weekly list stop being two different visual languages for one quantity. They become the same chart at two scales: today in hours, the week in days.
  • Nothing is coloured to mean bad. The curve is ink, the area under it is a tint, and amber marks the part that is above the threshold. Colour stops competing with the numbers.

What it costs

  • The chart is drawn from data the page already has, but the browser still has to draw it, and a screen reader gets a sentence instead of a picture. The values have to be repeated in text, which the readout row does.
  • Two thresholds on one axis is more than a first-time reader expects. The legend has to name both, and the reader's line only appears once they have set one.
  • It is the hardest of the three to build and the easiest to get wrong at small sizes. The curve has to stay legible at 320 pixels, where the week chart is 280 pixels wide.
05

The almanac

A forecast printed in an almanac: a masthead, a rule, and seven ruled rows with the figures set large in a right-hand column. There are no charts at all. The numbers are the chart, and the wet days are marked once in the gutter.

What it changes in the experience

  • The week becomes a column of figures. A reader scans the numbers down the right edge instead of reading seven rows of sentences, and Thursday and Friday stand out because they are the two large ones.
  • The heading stops being an emoji. The masthead is the mark and the name, and the sentence under it is the one thing the page has to say.
  • One mark per row, in the gutter. A filled block means the day reaches the umbrella threshold, and the block says the same thing the ☔ says today, only once per row and without a second meaning to learn.
  • It is the quietest of the three. Nothing pulses, nothing glows, and the page reads like a printed table you could pin up.

What it costs

  • It gives up the hourly strip, which is the only part of the page that says when. That has to be put back somewhere, and a printed page has no good place for it.
  • A sheet of pale paper on a dark photograph is the sharpest contrast of the three, and it is the one most likely to look like a different website pasted onto the page.
  • It is the least useful on a phone, where the week is the thing people scroll past and the next few hours are the thing they came for.
06

What every direction has to keep

These are the app's rules, not styling preferences. A redesign that breaks any of them has changed what the app says, not how it looks.

One threshold, one alert

The tab title, the favicon, the banner, the row mark, the notification and the calendar export all fire at the same UMBRELLA_THRESHOLD. The reader's own threshold is a second answer with its own words, and it never stands in for the app's advice.

Honest band names

Rain unlikely, rain possible, rain likely. A 25% day is not dry, and a probability does not predict an intensity. The name is always in the text as well as the colour.

Unknown is not zero

A day the service did not report gets its own band, is never counted as dry, and never appears in the calendar file or above a threshold line.

One address per city

Every way of asking for a forecast is redirected to /city/<slug>, and the canonical link, the sitemap and the structured data all point at the same address.

Right status codes

A missing city answers 404, a broken upstream answers 503, and a failed search on the start page stays 200. The error copy says what happened in plain English.

Reachable without a mouse

Every value in a chart is also in text, the hourly values are in a table as well as a strip, and the whole thing works with the script switched off.

07

Night street, with the mark from this page

Night street fixes the most: the answer stops sitting below the form, the hourly strip stops needing a hover, and the page stops being a white slab on top of a good photograph. The mark and the dark panel belong to the same idea, because both come out of the street in the picture.

What it would take

  • templates/index.html: the panel structure, the mark in the heading, the strip with its now rule, the week as columns. The Jinja loops stay the same, so the tests that check the markup keep working.
  • static/style.css: a new palette as custom properties, a new type stack, and the strip rewritten for the taller bars. The layout budget has to be measured again afterwards.
  • static/favicon.svg and static/favicon-rain.svg: the two new marks, and the constant in tools/make_icons.py with them.
  • app.py: the hourly summary sentence, because "rain from 06:00 to 10:00, and again from 15:00" is a new string and a new rule, and the rule belongs behind the test suite.

What to do first

  • Put the mark in place on its own. It is a change to two files and it is independent of any layout decision, and it makes every later screenshot read as one app.
  • Reorder one page: the city and the answer first, the form after. That is a template change, it costs no CSS, and it is the change a phone reader notices most.
  • Then take the dark panel, because it is the expensive half and the one that needs the vertical budget measured in a real browser.
